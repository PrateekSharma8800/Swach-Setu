Case Study · Indore, Madhya Pradesh

Indore: A Model for Smarter Waste Management

Understanding how citizen participation, waste segregation and scientific processing can help build cleaner cities.

Introduction Journey Workflow Practices Evidence Challenges Lessons Concepts Sources
Introduction

From Overflowing Bins to a National Example

Indore is the largest city of Madhya Pradesh and its commercial capital, located on the Malwa Plateau in central India.[3][5]

Before 2016, Indore faced the same waste problems as many growing cities: waste was mixed at home, collection was irregular, public bins overflowed and garbage was dumped in open areas and at the Devguradia trenching ground.[3][5]

The Indore Municipal Corporation (IMC), the city's local government, led the change. It ended a poorly performing private collection contract, started door-to-door collection, made segregation compulsory and invested in transfer stations and processing plants.[2][5]

This happened under the Swachh Bharat Mission, launched by the Government of India in 2014. Its cleanliness survey, Swachh Survekshan, ranks cities on waste collection, processing, sanitation and public awareness.[5]

Timeline

Indore's Journey Under Swachh Bharat Mission

Key milestones as reported by the sources. Dates differ slightly between some sources, as noted.

  1. 2014

    Swachh Bharat Mission launched

    The Government of India begins a national mission to end open defecation and improve solid waste management.[5]

  2. 2015

    A fresh start

    IMC terminates its contract with a private waste concessionaire whose financial problems had caused irregular collection.[2][3]

  3. 2015–16

    Pilot in two wards

    Door-to-door collection is tested in two wards (December 2015 according to NITI Aayog–CSE; January 2016 according to Smart City Indore and GMI).[3][2][5]

  4. 2016

    City-wide collection

    Door-to-door collection grows to 10 wards and then to all 85 wards by the end of 2016. In the first Swachh Survekshan survey (2016), Indore was ranked 25th.[3][5]

  5. 2017

    Cleanest city of India

    Indore is ranked first in Swachh Survekshan. Separate bins for sanitary and hazardous waste are added, and the Choithram Mandi bio-methanation plant is commissioned (December 2017).[3][2]

  6. 2017–22

    Six consecutive titles

    The CAG audit report records that Indore won the Cleanest City title for six consecutive years, 2017 to 2022.[4]

How it works

From Your Home to Processing: The Six Steps

Each step depends on the one before it. If waste is mixed at step 2, every later step becomes harder.

  1. 1 🚚

    Door-to-door collection

    Collection vehicles visit every household and shop on a fixed route, so people don't need to throw waste on the street or in open bins.

  2. 2 🗂️

    Segregation at source

    Households separate waste before handing it over: wet, dry, plastic, sanitary, domestic hazardous and e-waste.

  3. 3 🛣️

    Separate collection & transport

    Partitioned vehicles keep each type apart. At transfer stations, wet and dry waste go into separate containers, then to processing sites.

  4. 4 🌱

    Wet waste processing

    Food and organic waste is composted or sent to bio-methanation plants, where it is turned into biogas (bio-CNG) and manure.

  5. 5 ♻️

    Dry waste recovery

    Dry waste is sorted at Material Recovery Facilities into paper, plastic, metal and other recyclables, which are sold for recycling.

  6. 6 🏗️

    Scientific handling of residue

    Hazardous waste goes to a treatment facility. Only the leftover inert waste goes to engineered sanitary landfills instead of an open dump.

Based on the descriptions in sources [2][3][5].

Major practices

What Indore Does Differently

Seven practices that work together. None of them would succeed on its own.

🚚

Door-to-door collection

How: IMC studied the population and waste of each ward, then made route plans and assigned vehicles and staff to every ward. Vehicles are tracked by GPS from a command and control centre.[3][5]

Why it matters: Reliable, on-time collection built citizens' trust. The pilot showed that people were ready to segregate as long as collection was regular.[3]

🗂️

Source segregation

How: It began with two bins (green for wet, blue for dry). Separate bins for sanitary and hazardous waste were added in 2017. NITI Aayog–CSE (2021) reports six categories.[3]

Why it matters: Clean, separated waste can be composted or recycled. Mixed waste cannot be processed properly.

🍂

Wet waste processing

How: Wet waste is treated by aerobic composting and anaerobic digestion. NITI Aayog–CSE (2021) lists a composting plant at Devguradiya among the city's facilities.[3]

Why it matters: Organic waste rotting in a dump releases methane, a powerful greenhouse gas. Processing it produces compost instead.[5]

🔥

Bio-CNG & bio-methanation

How: In bio-methanation, wet waste is broken down without oxygen to make biogas, which is purified into bio-CNG. Indore has a smaller plant at Choithram Mandi and a large bio-CNG plant run as a public–private partnership.[2][5]

Why it matters: It turns kitchen and market waste into fuel, and the leftover digestate can be composted.[5]

🏭

Material Recovery Facilities (MRFs)

How: Dry waste is sorted into recyclables (plastic, metal, paper, cloth) and inert waste. Indore has two MRFs; one at the Devguradia trenching ground uses an automatic sorting system.[5]

Why it matters: Recovered materials are sold for recycling, and less waste reaches the landfill.

📣

Citizen awareness & participation

How: Campaigns such as "Do Bin Har Din" (two bins every day), street plays, radio jingles, school activities, religious and community leaders, and self-help groups of women. The "311" app lets citizens report problems.[3][5]

Why it matters: Segregation happens inside homes; it only works when people understand it and trust the system.

🏘️

Decentralised processing

How: Bulk waste generators must process their wet waste on-site or through private operators. Small composting units serve places such as schools, hotels, vegetable markets, temples and parks.[2][3]

Why it matters: Treating waste close to where it is produced reduces transport and the load on central plants.

A balanced view

Challenges and Lessons

Indore's system is widely praised, but it was built through years of effort and still needs continuous work.

⏪ Before the improvements

  • Segregation at source was almost nil; mixed waste was dumped at the Devguradiya trenching ground and on open land.[3]
  • Over a thousand garbage-vulnerable points existed in the city, and the Kahn river had become polluted with sewage.[3]
  • A private contractor's financial crisis caused irregular collection.[2][5]
  • A survey found residents were unhappy with civic services and distrustful of IMC.[5]

🔎 Issues raised by later audit

  • Testing commissioned by the CAG found microplastic particles in water samples from Pipaliyapala lake, Indore (1,765–2,175 particles per cubic metre), showing plastic pollution remains a concern.[4]
  • The CAG noted royalty of about ₹3.96 crore (excluding interest) pending recovery from the private operator of a material recovery facility — a reminder that contracts need close monitoring.[4]
  • Keeping a resource person on every collection vehicle to ensure segregation is described as cost-intensive.[3]

🤝 Public participation is essential

Sources agree that Indore succeeded because citizens, officials, waste workers and community leaders took part together. A system imposed only from the top would not last.[3][5]

🗂️ Segregation must be consistent

Processing plants only work well with clean, separated waste. Indore focused on achieving segregation before scaling up processing; it needs the same discipline every day.[3]

🛠️ Infrastructure and monitoring cost money

Vehicles, transfer stations, plants, GPS tracking and staff require steady funding — from user charges, fines, sale of products and government support — and careful contract management.[4][5]

Learning

What Can Other Indian Cities Learn?

Five practical lessons from Indore's experience.

  1. 1

    Segregate waste at source

    Make separation at home the first rule. It decides whether waste can be composted, recycled or safely treated later.

  2. 2

    Ensure reliable door-to-door collection

    Start with pilots, plan routes ward by ward and collect on time every day. Reliability builds trust.

  3. 3

    Process wet and dry waste separately

    Send wet waste to composting or bio-methanation and dry waste to material recovery, so that very little is left for landfill.

  4. 4

    Involve citizens and waste workers

    Run awareness campaigns, engage self-help groups and include informal waste workers in the system. The CAG audit found that, among the cities it audited, only Indore had engaged informal-sector workers (rag pickers and kabadiwalas) in its solid waste management.[4]

  5. 5

    Monitor collection and processing regularly

    Use GPS tracking, weighbridges, complaint apps and regular audits to find problems early and keep contractors accountable.

Concepts

How This Case Study Connects to Environmental Concepts

🏭

Environmental pollution

Open dumping and burning pollute air, soil and water. Indore's case shows how proper collection and processing reduce these sources of pollution.

🗑️

Solid waste management

A practical example of the full chain: generation, segregation, collection, transport, processing and disposal, as required by the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016.

🌍

Sustainable development

Meeting a growing city's needs while protecting health and resources for the future — for example by reducing methane from rotting waste.

👥

Public participation

Environmental protection works best when citizens, local government and communities share responsibility.

♻️

Resource recovery

Waste becomes a resource: compost from food waste, bio-CNG from organic waste and recyclables from dry waste.
